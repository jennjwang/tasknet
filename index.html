How TaskNet is built

Every task starts with workers describing their own jobs. Every task statement is then written to O*NET's standards and validated by job analysts and workers.

  1. Discover

    132 Worker interviews

    A worker's first account of a job is rarely complete, so we treat discovery as active elicitation: our AI interviewer helps workers recall their work, proposes missing tasks, and each worker confirms or revises the list.

  2. Aggregate

    2,726 Task reports

    Workers describe the same work in different words, so we group reports by goal and action rather than by vocabulary. Reports that differ only in tools or context become one task.

  3. Validate

    318 Raters, 7,923 Ratings

    O*NET Center analysts check every statement against O*NET's standards, and workers rate whether it reflects their job. Tasks rated relevant by at least a quarter of workers are kept.

What the ratings mean

318 workers rated the tasks, each rating up to 30. Relevance comes from everyone who rated a task, and the other ratings only from workers who said it belongs to their occupation. Tasks rated relevant by fewer than 25% were dropped.

Is It Part of the Job?

Relevance
Share of workers who say the task belongs to their occupation, not just their own role.
Importance
How important the task is to the occupation, averaged on O*NET's scale from 1 (not important) to 5 (extremely important).

How the Work Happens

Frequency
How often a worker typically does the task, from once a year or less to hourly. Shown as the nearest step on that scale.
Duration
How long one round of the task takes, from under 15 minutes to four or more workdays.

Expertise

Training
How much on-the-job and classroom training it takes to do the task well, from a short demonstration to over 10 years.
Top-worker gap
Share of workers who say a top performer (the best 10% in the same setting) clearly does the task better than an average worker.

Can It Be Specified and Checked?

Articulability
Share who say the standard for doing the task well can be clearly stated in advance. Low values point to tacit know-how.
Verifiability
Share who say a trained coworker could check whether the task was done correctly.
